2. Reading a list of numbers

What you will be able to do

  • Count how many values are in a list, counting every repeat.
  • Find the smallest and largest values, comparing place value and not just the first digit.
  • Compare decimals with different numbers of digits.
  • Check a copied list against the original, value by value.
  • Count values that meet a condition such as "at least 80."

Read a list one value at a time

Data often arrives as a list of numbers separated by commas. Three habits keep the reading accurate:

  • Count every value, repeats included. If 12 appears three times, it counts three times. Touch each value as you count, or count in groups of five.
  • Compare by place value. A three-digit whole number such as 108 is larger than any two-digit number such as 99, even though 9 is bigger than 1. For decimals, compare the whole-number parts first, then the tenths, then the hundredths. Writing 4.54 point 5 as 4.504 point 5 0 makes it easier to compare with 4.254 point 2 5.
  • Check copies in the same order. Line up the copy with the original and compare position by position. Swapped digits, such as 71 typed as 17, are easy to miss on a quick look.

Words that set a condition. "At least 80" means 80 or more, so 80 itself counts. "More than 80" leaves 80 out. "Less than 30" leaves 30 out, and "at most 30" includes it.

Worked example: A list of 11 values

Eleven friends each reported how many text messages they sent during a movie: 4, 12, 0, 7, 12, 3, 25, 7, 9, 12, 1. How many values are there, and what are the smallest and largest?

  1. Count: 4, 12, 0, 7, 12 (five), 3, 25, 7, 9, 12 (ten), 1 (eleven)Count in groups of five. The three 12s and the two 7s each count every time they appear.
  2. Smallest: 0Zero is a real value in this list. One friend sent no texts, and that still counts as a value.
  3. Largest: 25Scan for the values with the most digits first: 12, 12, 25 and 12. Of those, 25 is largest.

Answer: 11 values. The smallest is 0 and the largest is 25.

3. Evenly spaced numbers

What you will be able to do

  • Find the step between evenly spaced numbers by subtracting neighbors.
  • Subtract three-digit numbers, borrowing correctly.
  • Continue a pattern from a starting number, and find the 5th or 6th number.
  • Count how many numbers are in a list, not just how many steps.
  • Tell whether a list goes up by the same step every time.

Start, step and keep going

Scales on graphs and many tables use numbers that go up by the same amount each time, such as 40, 55, 70, 85. That amount is the step.

  • Find the step: subtract two neighbors, the larger minus the smaller. 55 − 40 = 1555 minus 40 equals 15. Check with another pair: 70 − 55 = 1570 minus 55 equals 15.
  • Keep going: add the step to the last number, again and again.
  • Count numbers, not gaps. A list always has one more number than it has steps, like fence posts and the gaps between them. From 40 to 85 by 15 there are 3 steps but 4 numbers.

Two slips to watch for

  • Subtracting the first and last numbers. 85 − 40 = 4585 minus 40 equals 45 covers three steps, not one.
  • Subtracting the smaller digit from the larger in every column. In 245 − 180245 minus 180, the tens column is 4 minus 8, which needs a borrow. Taking 8 minus 4 instead gives 145, which is wrong.

Worked example: Continue 12, 19, 26, 33

The list 12, 19, 26, 33 keeps going by the same step. Find the step and the next three numbers.

  1. 19 − 12 = 719 minus 12 equals 7Subtract two neighbors to find the step.
  2. 26 − 19 = 726 minus 19 equals 7Check with a second pair. The step is 7 both times.
  3. 33 + 7 = 40, 40 + 7 = 47, 47 + 7 = 5433 plus 7 equals 40, 40 plus 7 equals 47, 47 plus 7 equals 54Add the step to the last number, one at a time.

Answer: the step is 7, and the next three numbers are 40, 47 and 54. The full list 12, 19, 26, 33, 40, 47, 54 has 7 numbers and 6 steps.

4. Reading a bar graph

What you will be able to do

  • Tell the horizontal axis (x-axis) from the vertical axis (y-axis).
  • Find the step on an axis scale.
  • Read the height of a bar, including a bar that ends between gridlines.
  • Use axis labels and the title to say what a bar means.
  • Compare two bars by subtracting their heights.

Axes, scales and labels

  • The horizontal axis runs left to right along the bottom. It is also called the x-axis.
  • The vertical axis runs up and down the side. It is also called the y-axis.
  • The numbers along an axis form its scale. Find the scale's step by subtracting two neighboring numbers, as in Section 3. The step is often 2, 5, 10, 50 or 100, not 1.
  • The axis labels and the title say what is being counted or measured. Read them before reading any bar.

To read a bar, follow its top straight across to the vertical scale. If it ends between two gridlines, find the step and decide how far up it is: halfway between 40 and 50 is 45.

Two slips to watch for

  • Counting gridlines. A bar that reaches the 4th gridline on a scale of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 shows 200, not 4.
  • Reading the wrong line. Use the line the bar's top actually touches, and not the line just above or below it.

5. Units and amounts of time

What you will be able to do

  • Notice the unit that goes with each number, such as years, seconds or minutes.
  • Change minutes and seconds to seconds, and seconds to minutes.
  • Write an amount of time as a decimal number of minutes.
  • Find how many minutes pass between two clock times, including across noon.

Time does not work in tens

One minute is 60 seconds, and one hour is 60 minutes. Because time groups by 60 and not by 10 or 100, the digits in a time cannot be read like a decimal.

  • Minutes and seconds to seconds: multiply the minutes by 60, then add the extra seconds.
  • Seconds to minutes and seconds: take out as many groups of 60 as fit, and the rest are seconds.
  • Seconds to decimal minutes: divide by 60. So 30 seconds is half a minute, 0.50 point 5, not 0.300 point 3 0.
  • Time between clock times: count up to the next full hour, then count whole hours, then the extra minutes. Do not subtract clock times as if they were decimals.

Also keep a time of day, such as 11:00 AM, separate from an amount of time, such as 150 seconds.

Worked example: Seconds and clock times

(a) Write 205 seconds in minutes and seconds. (b) How many minutes pass from 11:40 AM to 12:25 PM?

  1. 205 − 60 − 60 − 60 = 25205 minus 60 minus 60 minus 60 equals 25Three groups of 60 fit in 205, with 25 seconds left, so 205 seconds is 3 minutes 25 seconds.
  2. 11:40 AM to 12:00 PM is 20 minutesCount up to the full hour first. 12:00 PM is noon.
  3. 12:00 PM to 12:25 PM is 25 minutesThen count the extra minutes and add: 20 plus 25 is 45.

Answer: (a) 3 minutes 25 seconds. (b) 45 minutes. Subtracting 1140 from 1225 as plain numbers would give 85, which is wrong, because an hour has 60 minutes, not 100.
