2. Comparing decimals

What you will be able to do

  • Compare two decimals that have different numbers of digits.
  • Fill in zeros so two decimals have the same number of places.
  • Tell when two decimals that look different are equal, such as 0.100 point 1 0 and 0.10 point 1.
  • Put a short list of decimals in order.

Line up the decimal points

To compare two decimals, line up their decimal points and fill in zeros at the end so both have the same number of digits after the point. Zeros added at the end do not change the value: 0.10 point 1, 0.100 point 1 0 and 0.1000 point 1 0 0 are the same number. Then compare digit by digit from the left. The first place where the digits differ decides which number is larger.

Comparing 0.00710 point 0 0 7 1 and 0.10 point 1 place by place
NumberOnesTenthsHundredthsThousandthsTen-thousandths
0.00710 point 0 0 7 100071
0.10 point 1 (written as 0.10000 point 1 0 0 0)01000

The tenths place is the first place where the digits differ: 0 against 1. So 0.10 point 1 is larger, even though 0.00710 point 0 0 7 1 has more digits.

Three slips to watch for

  • Longer is larger. Thinking 0.00710 point 0 0 7 1 is more than 0.10 point 1 because 71 is more than 1. More digits after the point does not make a number larger.
  • Shorter is larger. Thinking 0.50 point 5 is more than 0.750 point 7 5 because tenths are bigger pieces than hundredths. Fill in a zero: 0.500 point 5 0 is less than 0.750 point 7 5.
  • Skipping the zeros right after the point. 0.050 point 0 5 is 5 hundredths and 0.50 point 5 is 5 tenths, ten times as much. A zero right after the point changes the value.

Worked example: Which is larger, 0.0482 or 0.05?

Which is larger, 0.04820 point 0 4 8 2 or 0.050 point 0 5?

  1. 0.05 = 0.05000 point 0 5 equals 0 point 0 5 0 0Fill in zeros so both numbers have four digits after the point.
  2. Tenths: 0 and 0. Hundredths: 4 and 5.Compare from the left. The tenths match, so move to the hundredths. 5 is more than 4.

Answer: 0.050 point 0 5 is larger, even though 0.04820 point 0 4 8 2 has more digits.

3. Inequality symbols and phrases

What you will be able to do

  • Read the symbols <, >, ≤, ≥, = and ≠.
  • Read an inequality from either side, such as 9 < x9 is less than x and x > 9x is greater than 9.
  • Turn phrases such as "more than," "at least" and "no more than" into a symbol.
  • Decide whether the boundary number itself makes a statement true.

Six symbols

Six comparison symbols
SymbolRead it asA true example

≤less than or equal to symbolis less than or equal to4 ≤ 54 is less than or equal to 5 and 5 ≤ 55 is less than or equal to 5

The open side of < or > faces the larger number. You can read an inequality from either end: 3 < 83 is less than 8 and 8 > 38 is greater than 3 say the same thing. The line under ≤ and ≥ is the "or equal to" part.

Phrases and their symbols

  • "more than," "greater than," "above," "exceeds": >
  • "less than," "fewer than," "below": <
  • "at least," "no less than," "or more": ≥
  • "at most," "no more than," "or fewer": ≤
  • "equal to," "is": =
  • "not equal to," "is not," "is different from": ≠
The boundary number. "More than 30" leaves 30 out: 31 counts, but 30 does not. "At least 30" lets 30 in.

Three slips to watch for

  • Dropping "or equal to." 5 ≤ 55 is less than or equal to 5 is true, because ≤ allows the two sides to be equal.
  • Flipping the direction. 9 < x9 is less than x means x is more than 9. Reading it as "x is less than 9" turns it around.
  • Counting the boundary. 30 is not "more than 30." It is equal to 30.

Worked example: A weight limit

A bridge allows trucks that weigh at most 12 tons. Write this with w for the weight in tons, and decide whether a truck that weighs exactly 12 tons is allowed.

  1. "at most 12" means 12 or lessThe weight can be below 12 or equal to 12, so the symbol is ≤.
  2. w ≤ 12w is less than or equal to 12Write the variable, the symbol and the number.
  3. 12 ≤ 1212 is less than or equal to 12Check the boundary: 12 is equal to 12, and ≤ allows equal, so this is true.

Answer: w ≤ 12w is less than or equal to 12, and a 12-ton truck is allowed.

4. Rounding to two or three decimal places

What you will be able to do

  • Round to two decimal places and to three decimal places.
  • Round a negative number.
  • Carry correctly when a 9 rounds up.
  • Avoid rounding in steps from the right.

How to round

  1. Find the last digit you will keep. For two decimal places, that is the hundredths digit, the second digit after the point. For three decimal places, it is the thousandths digit, the third digit after the point.
  2. Look at the single digit right after it.
  3. If that digit is 5 or more, add 1 to the kept digit. If it is 4 or less, leave the kept digit alone.
  4. Drop every digit after the kept digit.

Negative numbers. Round the digits as if the number were positive, then put the minus sign back. −1.476negative 1 point 4 7 6 to two decimal places is −1.48negative 1 point 4 8.

Zeros at the end. A rounded answer can end in zero. 2.49682 point 4 9 6 8 to two decimal places is 2.502 point 5 0. Typing 2.52 point 5 names the same number, but 2.502 point 5 0 shows the two decimal places.

Three slips to watch for

  • Rounding in steps from the right. Only the one digit right after the kept digit matters. −0.71549negative 0 point 7 1 5 4 9 to three decimal places is −0.715negative 0 point 7 1 5. Rounding to −0.7155negative 0 point 7 1 5 5 first and then to −0.716negative 0 point 7 1 6 is a slip.
  • Rounding a negative number the other way. −1.476negative 1 point 4 7 6 to two decimal places is −1.48negative 1 point 4 8, not −1.47negative 1 point 4 7. The digits round the same way they would for 1.4761 point 4 7 6.
  • A 9 that rounds up. When a 9 goes up by 1, it becomes 0 and adds 1 to the digit on its left. 1.49681 point 4 9 6 8 to two decimal places is 1.501 point 5 0.

Worked example: Round −2.3651 two ways

Round −2.3651negative 2 point 3 6 5 1 to two decimal places, and then to three decimal places.

  1. Two decimal places: keep the 6, look at the 5The 5 is 5 or more, so the 6 becomes 7. Put the minus sign back: −2.37negative 2 point 3 7.
  2. Three decimal places: keep the 5, look at the 1The 1 is 4 or less, so the 5 stays: −2.365negative 2 point 3 6 5.

Answer: −2.37negative 2 point 3 7 to two decimal places and −2.365negative 2 point 3 6 5 to three decimal places.

Directions decide the answer. "Two decimal places" means exactly two digits after the point. "Three decimal places" means exactly three. Read the direction before you round.

5. Numbers in sentences and lists

What you will be able to do

  • Match each number in a sentence to what it describes.
  • Count the values in a list, including values that repeat.
  • Read units written with symbols, such as mm Hg and µg/gmicrograms per gram.

Label every number first

Word problems often pack several numbers into one sentence. Before you type anything into a calculator, label each number with what it describes and its unit.

When you count a list, every value counts, even one that repeats. Count in groups of five, or cross values off as you go. In a list separated by commas, the count is one more than the number of commas.

Units with symbols. A slash or the word "per" means "for each" or "divided by," so g/L is grams per liter. In a unit, the prefix µmu (the Greek letter mu) means micro, or one millionth, so µg/gmicrograms per gram is micrograms per gram. The unit mm Hg is millimeters of mercury, a unit for pressure.

Worked example: Label the numbers

A bakery packed 36 boxes of muffins. Each box held 12 muffins and weighed 2.752 point 7 5 pounds. The bakery's goal was at least 40 boxes. Label each number.
