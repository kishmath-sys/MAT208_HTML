2. Percents as decimals

What you will be able to do

  • Change a percent to a decimal, including percents such as 99.5%99 point 5 percent.
  • Change a decimal to a percent.
  • Tell when a number in a problem is a percent and when it is an amount that stays as it is.

Percent means "out of 100"

The word percent means "per hundred," so 95% means 95 out of 100, which is the decimal 0.950 point 9 5. Moving between the two forms comes down to dividing or multiplying by 100.

  • Percent to decimal: divide by 100. That moves the decimal point two places to the left. Fill any empty places with zeros.
  • Decimal to percent: multiply by 100. That moves the decimal point two places to the right, then add the percent sign.

9%0.090 point 0 9A single-digit percent needs a zero right after the point

Only percents get converted

A number with a percent sign changes form when a box asks for a decimal. A number with units, such as 3 inches, 15 pounds or 12 minutes, is an amount, not a percent. It stays exactly as it is.

Three slips to watch for

  • Reading the digit as the percent. 0.90 point 9 is 9 tenths, which is 90 hundredths, so it is 90%, not 9%. And 9% is 0.090 point 0 9.
  • Moving only one place. 99.5%99 point 5 percent is 0.9950 point 9 9 5, not 9.959 point 9 5. Count two places to the left.
  • Converting an amount. 3 inches stays 3. Writing 0.030 point 0 3 treats it as if it were 3%.

Worked example: Write 92.5% as a decimal

Write 92.5%92 point 5 percent as a decimal.

  1. 92.5 ÷ 10092 point 5 divided by 100Percent to decimal means divide by 100.
  2. 92.5 → 9.25 → 0.92592 point 5, then 9 point 2 5, then 0 point 9 2 5Move the decimal point one place left, then a second place left.

Answer: 0.9250 point 9 2 5 (not 9.259 point 2 5, which would be 925%925 percent)

3. Rounding to two decimal places

What you will be able to do

  • Round to two decimal places.
  • Round to one decimal place.
  • Carry correctly when a 9 rounds up.
  • Write the zeros that two decimal places calls for.

How to round

  1. Find the last digit you will keep. For two decimal places, that is the second digit after the point (the hundredths digit). For one decimal place, it is the first digit after the point (the tenths digit).
  2. Look at the single digit right after it.
  3. If that digit is 5 or more, add 1 to the kept digit. If it is 4 or less, leave the kept digit alone.
  4. Drop every digit after the kept digit.

Four slips to watch for

  • Chopping instead of rounding. 37.866137 point 8 6 6 1 to two decimal places is 37.8737 point 8 7, not 37.8637 point 8 6. The third digit, 6, is 5 or more.
  • Rounding in steps from the right. Only the one digit right after the kept digit matters. 52.344952 point 3 4 4 9 to two decimal places is 52.3452 point 3 4. Rounding to 52.34552 point 3 4 5 first and then to 52.3552 point 3 5 is a slip.
  • A 9 that rounds up. When a 9 goes up by 1, it becomes 0 and adds 1 to the digit on its left. 6.9976 point 9 9 7 to two decimal places is 7.007 point 0 0.
  • Dropping the zeros. Two decimal places means two digits after the point, even when they are zeros, as in 7.007 point 0 0.

Worked example: Round 47.38461 two ways

Round 47.3846147 point 3 8 4 6 1 to two decimal places, and then to one decimal place.

  1. Two decimal places: keep the 8, look at the 4The 4 is 4 or less, so the 8 stays: 47.3847 point 3 8.
  2. One decimal place: keep the 3, look at the 8The 8 is 5 or more, so the 3 becomes 4: 47.447 point 4.

Answer: 47.3847 point 3 8 to two decimal places and 47.447 point 4 to one decimal place.

Directions decide the answer. "Round to two decimal places" means exactly two digits after the point. "Round to one decimal place" means exactly one. "Do not round" means type every digit.

4. Comparing decimals and "between" notation

What you will be able to do

  • Compare decimals that have different numbers of digits.
  • Read inequality notation such as 12.5 < x < 14.2512 point 5 is less than x is less than 14 point 2 5.
  • Read interval notation such as (12.5, 14.25)the interval from 12 point 5 to 14 point 2 5.
  • Decide whether a number is between two others.

Line up the decimal points

To compare two decimals, line up the decimal points and give both numbers the same number of digits after the point by adding zeros at the end. Then compare digit by digit from the left. More digits does not mean larger: 4.84 point 8 is 4.804 point 8 0, which is larger than 4.754 point 7 5.

"Between," written two ways

  • Inequality notation: 12.5 < x < 14.2512 point 5 is less than x is less than 14 point 2 5 says x is greater than 12.512 point 5 and less than 14.2514 point 2 5. The smaller number goes on the left.
  • Interval notation: (12.5, 14.25)the interval from 12 point 5 to 14 point 2 5 says the same thing: the smaller number, a comma, then the larger number, inside parentheses. It stands for every number between the two ends, not just the two numbers, and it is not a point on a graph.

Three slips to watch for

  • Longer is larger. 2.152 point 1 5 has more digits than 2.32 point 3, but 2.302 point 3 0 is larger than 2.152 point 1 5.
  • Reading the digits as whole numbers. Comparing 0.50 point 5 with 0.450 point 4 5 as "5 against 45" gets it backward. 0.500 point 5 0 is larger than 0.450 point 4 5.
  • Reversing the order. In both notations the smaller number comes first.

Worked example: Is a 1.5 pound loaf accepted?

A bakery accepts a loaf of bread if its weight w, in pounds, satisfies 1.45 < w < 1.61 point 4 5 is less than w is less than 1 point 6. In interval notation that is (1.45, 1.6)the interval from 1 point 4 5 to 1 point 6. Is a loaf that weighs 1.51 point 5 pounds accepted?

  1. 1.45, 1.50, 1.601 point 4 5, 1 point 5 0, 1 point 6 0Give all three numbers two digits after the point.
  2. 1.45 < 1.501 point 4 5 is less than 1 point 5 045 hundredths is less than 50 hundredths, so the loaf is above the lower end.
  3. 1.50 < 1.601 point 5 0 is less than 1 point 6 0It is also below the upper end.

Answer: Yes. 1.51 point 5 is between 1.451 point 4 5 and 1.61 point 6.

5. Reading the numbers in a problem

What you will be able to do

  • Match each number in a sentence to what it describes, using its unit and the words around it.
  • Copy a list of numbers without leaving one out.
  • Type "two times" a number on one line using *.

Label every number first

Word problems often pack five or six numbers into one or two sentences. Before you use any of them, go through the sentence and label each number with its unit and what it describes. Then use the labels, not the order the numbers appear in, to decide which number goes where.

On one line, a calculator or an online form uses * for times, so "two times 7.257 point 2 5" is typed 2 * 7.252 star 7 point 2 5.

Three slips to watch for

  • Grabbing the first number. The first number in a sentence is often a count, not the measurement you need.
  • Mixing up two numbers with the same unit. When two numbers are both in pounds or both in minutes, read the words right before each one.
  • Leaving out a value when copying a list. Count the values in the problem and in your copy, and check that they match.

Worked example: A delivery van

A delivery van makes 48 stops a day, drives 9.69 point 6 miles between stops and spends 4 minutes at each stop. Its fuel tank holds 22.522 point 5 gallons. A form asks for two times the minutes spent at each stop.

  1. 48 stops, 9.69 point 6 miles, 4 minutes, 22.522 point 5 gallonsLabel each number: 48 is a count of stops, 9.69 point 6 is a distance, 4 is a time and 22.522 point 5 is the size of the tank.
  2. Minutes at each stop: 4Only one number is in minutes.
  3. 2 * 4 = 82 star 4 equals 8Type two times the number with *.

Answer: 8 minutes, typed as 2 * 42 star 4
