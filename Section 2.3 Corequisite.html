2. Reading a scale

What you will be able to do

  • Find the step between tick marks from two labels.
  • Read the value at a tick mark that has no label.
  • Read a point that sits halfway between two labels.
  • Work with steps other than 1, such as 2, 5 or 0.20 point 2.

Count the spaces, not the tick marks

An axis on a graph works like a number line. The tick marks are evenly spaced, and every space between two neighboring tick marks stands for the same amount, called the step. Often only some tick marks have labels.

  1. Pick two labeled tick marks and subtract their labels.
  2. Count the spaces between those two tick marks.
  3. Divide: the step is the difference between the labels divided by the number of spaces.
  4. To read a tick mark, start at a label and add one step for each space you move to the right.

Three slips to watch for

  • Assuming each tick mark is 1. The step can be 2, 5, 10, 0.50 point 5 or anything else. Work it out from the labels every time.
  • Counting tick marks instead of spaces. From one label to the next, there is always one more tick mark than there are spaces, because both labeled tick marks get counted.
  • Counting only the unlabeled tick marks. Three unlabeled tick marks between two labels make four spaces, not three.

Worked example: Read the dot on a number line

What value is the dot on?

  1. 120 − 100 = 20120 minus 100 equals 20Subtract the two labels.
  2. 4 spacesFrom 100 to 120 there are 5 tick marks, so there are 4 spaces between them.
  3. 20 ÷ 4 = 520 divided by 4 equals 5The step is 5. Each space stands for 5.
  4. 100 + 3 × 5 = 115100 + 3 times 5 equals 115The dot is 3 spaces to the right of 100.

Answer: 115

3. Reading and checking a list of numbers

What you will be able to do

  • Count the values in a list or a table, even when a row of the table is not full.
  • Find the smallest and largest values.
  • Count the values that are "greater than," "less than," "at least" or "at most" a number.
  • Put decimals in order.
  • Compare a typed copy of a list with the original to find a typing slip.

Reading a list carefully

  • Counting. Tick off each value as you count it, and count in groups of five or ten. In a table, count each column (or each row) and add, so a short row is not counted as full.
  • Condition words. "Greater than 75" leaves out 75 itself. "At least 75" includes 75. In the same way, "less than" leaves the number out and "at most" includes it.
  • Ordering decimals. Line up the decimal points and compare place by place from the left. Adding a zero can help: 2.42 point 4 is the same as 2.402 point 4 0, which is larger than 2.052 point 0 5.
  • Checking a typed copy. Compare the copy with the original one value at a time, in order. Two digits that trade places, such as 47 and 74, are an easy slip to miss.

Three slips to watch for

  • Off by one. Skipping a value or counting one twice. Counting in groups makes this easier to catch.
  • Rows times columns. A table with 5 rows and 3 columns holds 15 values only if every cell is filled.
  • Longer looks larger. 2.052 point 0 5 is not larger than 2.42 point 4 just because it has more digits.

Worked example: Wait times at a clinic

The wait times, in minutes, for 11 patients were: 12, 7, 15, 7, 9, 21, 12, 7, 18, 10, 9. How many values are there, what are the smallest and largest, and how many are at least 12 minutes?

  1. 12, 7, 15, 7, 9 | 21, 12, 7, 18, 10 | 9Count in groups of five: 5, then 10, then 11 values.
  2. Smallest 7, largest 21Scan the whole list once for each. The 7 appears three times, but the smallest value is still 7.
  3. 12, 15, 21, 12, 18"At least 12" includes both 12s. That makes 5 values.

Answer: 11 values; smallest 7, largest 21; 5 values are at least 12.

4. Comparing two amounts

What you will be able to do

  • Find how much more one amount is than another by subtracting.
  • Find how many times as large one amount is as another by dividing.
  • Choose subtraction or division from the wording of a question.
  • Put the right amount on top when you divide.

Two ways to compare

  • How much more? Subtract: larger amount minus smaller amount. The answer has the same units as the amounts, such as dollars or miles.
  • How many times as large? Divide: the amount being described divided by the amount it is compared with. The answer has no units. A result of 2 means twice as large, and a result of 1 means the two are equal.

The same pair of numbers gives two different answers, so read the question for "how much more" or "how many times."

Three slips to watch for

  • Subtracting when the question says "times." "How many times as much" always means divide.
  • Flipping the division. If the amount being described is the larger one, the answer must be more than 1. An answer less than 1 means the division is upside down.
  • Treating percents differently. To compare 60% with 40%, divide the percent numbers just like any other amounts: 60 ÷ 40 = 1.560 divided by 40 equals 1 point 5.

Worked example: Two gym memberships

Gym A costs $45 a month and Gym B costs $30 a month. How much more does Gym A cost, and how many times as much as Gym B does Gym A cost?

  1. 45 − 30 = 1545 minus 30 equals 15"How much more" means subtract. Gym A costs $15 more.
  2. 45 ÷ 30 = 1.545 divided by 30 equals 1 point 5"How many times as much" means divide. Gym A is the amount being described, so it goes on top.
  3. Check: 1.5 is more than 1Gym A costs more, so the answer should be more than 1. It is.

Answer: $15 more, and 1.51 point 5 times as much

5. Rounding large numbers and results

What you will be able to do

  • Round a whole number to the nearest thousand or the nearest hundred.
  • Carry correctly when a 9 rounds up.
  • Round each amount first when a question says to, then divide.
  • Round a result to two decimal places.

How to round

  1. Find the last digit you will keep. For the nearest thousand, that is the thousands digit. For two decimal places, it is the hundredths digit, the second digit after the point.
  2. Look at the single digit right after it.
  3. If that digit is 5 or more, add 1 to the kept digit. If it is 4 or less, leave the kept digit alone.
  4. In a whole number, replace every digit after the kept digit with 0. After a decimal point, drop them.

Four slips to watch for

  • Chopping instead of rounding. 26,700 to the nearest thousand is 27,000, not 26,000. The 7 after the kept digit means round up.
  • Rounding in steps from the right. 14,482 to the nearest thousand is 14,000. Rounding to 14,480, then 14,500, then 15,000 is a slip. Only the hundreds digit, 4, matters.
  • A 9 that rounds up. When a 9 goes up by 1, it becomes 0 and adds 1 to the digit on its left. 59,800 to the nearest thousand is 60,000.
  • Looking at the wrong digit. For the nearest thousand, the deciding digit is the hundreds digit, one place to the right of the thousands digit.

Worked example: Round 47,582 two ways

Round 47,582 to the nearest thousand, and then to the nearest hundred.

  1. Nearest thousand: keep the 7, look at the 5The 5 is 5 or more, so the 7 becomes 8, and the digits after it become zeros: 48,000.
  2. Nearest hundred: keep the 5, look at the 8The 8 is 5 or more, so the 5 becomes 6: 47,600.

Answer: 48,000 to the nearest thousand and 47,600 to the nearest hundred.

Directions decide the answer. "Nearest thousand" ends in three zeros. "Two decimal places" means exactly two digits after the point. When a question says to round each amount first, round before you do anything else.
