2. Ordered pairs and the coordinate plane

What you will be able to do

  • Read an ordered pair (x, y)x comma y as x first, then y.
  • Plot a point that has negative coordinates.
  • Read the coordinates of a point on a grid.
  • Turn a two-row table into ordered pairs.

x first, then y

An ordered pair (x, y)x comma y gives two moves that start at the origin, (0, 0)the point 0 comma 0, where the axes cross.

  • The first number, x, is the move across. Positive means right, and negative means left.
  • The second number, y, is the move up or down. Positive means up, and negative means down.
  • Order matters. (2, 5)the point 2 comma 5 and (5, 2)the point 5 comma 2 are two different points.

Three slips to watch for

  • Swapping the numbers. Going up first and across second plots (−3, 4)the point negative 3 comma 4 as if it were (4, −3)the point 4 comma negative 3.
  • Dropping a minus sign. (2, −5)the point 2 comma negative 5 is below the horizontal axis. Without the minus sign it lands above it.
  • Pairing the wrong numbers from a table. The two numbers in one pair come from the same column of the table, one from each row.

3. Negative numbers

What you will be able to do

  • Compare two negative numbers and order a list that mixes negative and positive numbers.
  • Place negative numbers on a vertical axis.
  • Find how far apart two temperatures are when one is below zero.
  • Add a rise in temperature to a temperature below zero.

Below zero

On a number line, numbers get larger as you move right. On a vertical axis, they get larger as you move up. Zero sits between the negative and positive numbers.

  • A negative number farther from zero is smaller. −30negative 30 is less than −5negative 5, the same way −30°Fnegative 30 degrees Fahrenheit is colder than −5°Fnegative 5 degrees Fahrenheit.
  • To find how far apart two numbers are, subtract the smaller from the larger. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding: 14 − (−9) = 14 + 9 = 2314 minus negative 9 equals 14 plus 9 equals 23.
  • Another way: count from the lower number up to 0, then from 0 up to the higher number, and add the two counts.

Three slips to watch for

  • Reading only the digits. 27 is more than 19, but −27negative 27 is less than −19negative 19.
  • Subtracting the digits when zero is crossed. From −9negative 9 to 14 is not 5 degrees. It is 9 degrees up to 0, then 14 more: 23 degrees.
  • Losing the minus sign. A temperature of −8negative 8 written as 8 lands on the wrong side of zero.

Worked example: Order five numbers

Put 6, −15negative 15, 0, −2negative 2 and −40negative 40 in order from least to greatest.

  1. Negatives first: −40negative 40, −15negative 15, −2negative 2Among the negatives, the one farthest from zero is the smallest, so −40negative 40 comes first.
  2. Then 0, then 6Zero is larger than every negative number and smaller than every positive number.

Answer: −40negative 40, −15negative 15, −2negative 2, 0, 6

4. Reading paired data tables and units

What you will be able to do

  • Find the value paired with another value in a two-row table.
  • Read units in a heading, such as "thousands of feet."
  • Work with a table whose values are not listed in order.
  • Spot a value that was most likely typed wrong.

One column, one pair

In a two-row table of paired data, each column is one pair. The value on top and the value under it belong together, so keep them together when you read or copy the table.

  • Units in the heading tell you what each number means. If a heading says "(thousands of feet)," then 8 means 8 thousand feet, which is 8,000 feet.
  • Values are not always in order. The smallest value can sit in any column, so search the whole row.
  • Scan for a value that does not fit. A number far from the others, such as one with a misplaced decimal point or a missing minus sign, is worth a second look.

Three slips to watch for

  • Reading from the wrong column. Slide straight down from the value you found, not one column over.
  • Dropping the units. "(thousands of feet)" changes 8 into 8,000.
  • Assuming the first column holds the smallest value. Check every column first.

5. Axis scales

What you will be able to do

  • Find the distance between the marks on an axis.
  • Find the value of a point that sits between labeled marks.
  • Choose a scale that covers the smallest and largest values, including values below zero.
  • Read a point when only some of the gridlines are labeled.

Reading a scale

  • Step size. Subtract two labels that are next to each other. Marks at 20, 40 and 60 are 20 apart.
  • Between marks. Halfway between two marks is the number halfway between their labels. If gridlines are not labeled, find what one gridline is worth first: the step between labels divided by the number of spaces between them.
  • Choosing a scale. Find the smallest and largest values. Start at a mark at or below the smallest value, end at a mark at or above the largest, and keep the marks evenly spaced. Values below zero need room below 0.

Three slips to watch for

  • Using a label as the step. Marks at 2,000 and 2,500 are 500 apart, not 2,000.
  • Counting each gridline as 1. If labels are 20 apart with 4 spaces between them, each gridline is worth 5.
  • Leaving out the negatives. A scale from 0 to 50 cannot show −38negative 38.

Worked example: Choose a scale for values from 12 to 87

A set of values runs from 12 to 87. Choose marks 10 apart that show every value.

  1. Smallest value 12: start at 1010 is the first mark at or below 12.
  2. Largest value 87: end at 9090 is the first mark at or above 87.
  3. Marks: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90Every step is 10, so the spacing is even.

Answer: marks from 10 to 90, every 10

6. Rising and falling lines: the sign of a slope

What you will be able to do

  • Read a line from left to right and say whether it rises, falls or is flat.
  • Find the slope between two points: the change in y divided by the change in x.
  • Match the sign of a slope to the direction of the line.
  • Subtract carefully when the points have negative coordinates.

Slope and direction

The slope between two points (x₁, y₁)x 1 comma y 1 and (x₂, y₂)x 2 comma y 2 is the change in y divided by the change in x:

slope = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)slope equals, open parenthesis y 2 minus y 1 close parenthesis, divided by, open parenthesis x 2 minus x 1 close parenthesis

  • Read every line from left to right.
  • A positive slope means the line rises from left to right.
  • A negative slope means the line falls from left to right.
  • A slope of 0 means the line is flat.

Three slips to watch for

  • Flipping the fraction. The change in y goes on top. The change in x goes on the bottom.
  • Mixing the order. Use the same point first on top and on the bottom. Mixing them gives the wrong sign.
  • Judging by the numbers instead of the direction. A line can sit below the axis, where every y-value is negative, and still rise. The sign of the slope comes from the direction.

  2. Change in x: 5 − 1 = 45 minus 1 equals 4Second x minus first x, in the same order.
  3. Slope: −8 ÷ 4 = −2negative 8 divided by 4 equals negative 2A negative slope, so the line falls from left to right.
